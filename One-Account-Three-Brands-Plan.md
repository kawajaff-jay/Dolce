# One Account, Three Brands — Concept & Build Plan
### Dolce Aesthetic Clinic · Polished by Dolce · Core Yoga & Pilates Studio

Prepared for Kawa, August 2026 — last updated September 2026

## The idea in one paragraph

Three businesses under one owner — a medical aesthetics clinic, a hair and nail salon, and a yoga and pilates studio — share one client-facing app and website instead of three separate ones. A client creates a single account, and from a "choose your destination" home screen moves fluidly between Dolce, Polished by Dolce, and Core: browsing services and prices, requesting bookings, chatting with an AI assistant for recommendations, earning and spending loyalty points across all three brands, and buying retail products. Behind the scenes, one admin dashboard gives you a single view of bookings, customers, revenue, and promotions across all three businesses, so you are not juggling three separate systems to run what is really one client relationship.

This is the strongest part of the concept: most beauty and wellness apps only show a client what one location sells. Yours can say "you booked a Hydrafacial at Dolce last month, your skin type suggests you'd also benefit from a LED Therapy add-on, and Core has a beginner Pilates class this week that pairs well with your recovery plan." That cross-brand intelligence is the actual product, not the booking calendar.

## How a client experiences it

The journey mirrors what's in your mockup, and it holds up well: a client opens the app, picks a destination (Dolce, Polished, or Core), explores services with clear pricing and categories (Skincare, Injectables, Body for Dolce; similar category splits for the others), taps into a service detail screen with duration, price, and what to expect, and requests a booking. The prototype now shows real self-service booking rather than only a WhatsApp handoff: the client picks from a rolling seven-day window of dates and a grid of time slots, already-booked slots for that brand show as struck-through and can't be selected, and tapping "Confirm Booking" asks for a name and a phone number and logs the request immediately — no account and no WhatsApp required. WhatsApp is still there as a second, equally live channel: "Also Message via WhatsApp" opens a real WhatsApp chat with the exact same service, date, and time pre-filled, so a client who prefers messaging gets the identical availability picture as the one shown in the app, and either path lands the same request in the same queue. This is a meaningfully bigger step than the original plan, which treated a WhatsApp-only handoff as the first-version compromise — this in-app picker still isn't a full staff-calendar integration (it checks slots against existing bookings in the shared data, not against individual staff schedules), but it removes the biggest reason clients would default to WhatsApp: not being able to see what's actually open.

Layered on top of browsing and booking is the AI assistant, reachable from every screen, which a client can ask things like "what's the best treatment for acne scars" or "I want to improve my skin and prepare for a wedding," and which responds with a short, personalized plan and a ranked list of recommended services pulled from real service data and, where the client has an account history, from their past bookings. Loyalty points accumulate from any purchase or booking across all three brands and can be redeemed for free treatments, exclusive discounts, gifts, or priority booking, which is what gives clients a reason to try the second and third brand rather than staying loyal to just one. A retail shop lets clients buy the product lines each brand carries (skin serums, hair and nail treatments, yoga mats and wellness goods) without a booking. An events and offers feed surfaces seasonal campaigns like a "Glow Season" facial discount at Dolce or a bridal package at Polished. All of this is available in English, Kurdish (Sorani, right-to-left, matching how it is written in Erbil), and Arabic, which matters a great deal for reach in the Kurdistan Region and wider Iraq.

The demo phone in the prototype is also no longer confined to that small on-page mockup — an "Open Full-Screen App" button expands it to fill the whole browser window, with an exit button (or the Escape key) to return to the marketing page. This is a small addition but it changes how the demo feels: instead of poking at a shrunk-down phone graphic embedded in a webpage, you get something much closer to what a client would actually experience opening the real app on their phone, which is worth using the next time you're showing this to someone.

## Client accounts, and booking without one

This section has changed more than any other since it was first written, and it is worth reading in full because the conclusion is close to the opposite of where it started.

The original plan was phone-first accounts: a client enters their number, receives a code, and is logged in. That was built, and for a while it worked as a demonstration — but only as a demonstration. There was no SMS service behind it, so the app printed the code on the screen. Anyone could type it and open anyone else's account, read their bookings and see their phone number. That is fine while one person is testing on their own laptop and completely unacceptable on a public website, so the client login has been **switched off**. Nobody can log in as a client today, and any session left over from the demo is cleared when the app opens.

What replaced it is better suited to the business anyway: **booking without an account at all**. A client opens a treatment, picks a date and a time, and is asked for the two things reception would ask for on the phone — a name and a number — plus an optional tick giving permission to send them aftercare advice. They press Confirm and the request lands in the Admin App exactly like any other booking. No password, nothing to forget, nothing to verify, nothing to pay for. Their details are remembered on their own phone so a returning client does not type them again, which is a convenience rather than an account: it never leaves their device and none of it reaches the clinic. The form is translated into Kurdish and Arabic alongside English, and the Kurdish lays out right-to-left correctly.

The honest cost of this is that a client cannot see their own booking history, because there is no account for that history to hang on. The Bookings tab tells them so and offers to connect them with the team instead. Whether that gap is worth closing is a live decision with real money attached. There are two credible routes. **Email sign-up** can be built immediately and costs nothing, but a salon client in Erbil is being asked for something they may not check, and with email confirmation currently switched off (it has to be, because staff accounts use internal addresses that cannot receive mail) their address would not be verified. **WhatsApp login** fits how people in Erbil actually communicate and is the natural choice on those grounds — but it requires Meta's WhatsApp Business API, which means a verified business account, an approved message template and a paid messaging provider, several weeks of approvals, and, from 1 October 2026, roughly 13 US cents per login code to an Iraqi number, up from under one cent before that date.

The recommendation, for now, is to leave it as it is and let real customers answer the question. Guest booking already removes the reason most people wanted an account, and the aftercare messages described below work without one. If customers start asking to see their own history, that is the signal to spend the weeks and the money — and by then you will know roughly how many logins a month you would be paying for, which today is guesswork.

## What the AI assistant should actually do — and where to be careful

Given the mix of a medical aesthetics clinic and two wellness businesses, the assistant needs two different postures depending on which brand a client is asking about. For Polished and Core, it can behave like a confident retail concierge: recommend services, build bundles, nudge upsells, and answer FAQs about hours, pricing, and staff. For Dolce, because it touches skin treatments, injectables, and medical procedures, the assistant should stay in a "here are options worth discussing with your provider" register rather than diagnosing conditions or promising outcomes — it should always route anything that sounds like a real medical question or contraindication toward booking a consultation, never resolve it in chat. This is both a safety issue and a liability one, and it is worth being deliberate about from the first version rather than retrofitting it later.

Technically, this kind of assistant is best built as a language model (Claude works well here) given a curated knowledge base of your actual services, prices, policies, and staff — a technique often called retrieval-augmented generation, where the assistant looks up real facts about your business before answering rather than guessing. On top of that, giving the assistant read access to a client's own booking history and stated preferences (skin type, goals, past treatments) lets it personalize recommendations the way the mockup shows, and giving it the ability to draft a booking request (which the client still confirms) closes the loop between "the AI suggested it" and "the client booked it." None of this requires the assistant to have access to other clients' data or anything outside your own service catalog, which keeps both privacy and cost under control.

## Recommended technical approach

Because this is one owner running three related but distinct businesses, and because the booking flow deliberately hands off to WhatsApp rather than needing a live scheduling engine, you do not need the most expensive version of "custom app with native iOS and Android development" to get a real, professional product live. A pragmatic path: build the client-facing product as a single responsive web app (a "progressive web app," which installs to a phone's home screen and works like an app without needing App Store or Play Store approval for the first version), wrap it in a thin native shell later once you want App Store presence, and keep the admin dashboard as an ordinary web app that you and your staff use from a laptop or tablet. This gets you a real product in front of clients fastest, at the lowest cost, and native app wrapping is a relatively small, well-understood step to add afterward rather than a rebuild.

For the backend, a managed platform (options like Supabase or Firebase) handles client accounts, service and pricing data, loyalty point balances, order history for the shop, and the multi-language content, without you needing to run and maintain your own servers — this matters a lot for a solo or small-team build because it removes an entire category of ongoing engineering work. WhatsApp booking is implemented either as a simple "click to open WhatsApp with a pre-filled message" link (essentially free and works immediately) or, once volume grows, as a proper WhatsApp Business Platform integration that can also send automated booking confirmations and reminders. The AI assistant runs on the Claude API, given access to your service catalog and, with the client's consent, their own booking history, with clear guardrails on the clinic side as described above. The admin dashboard is a straightforward web app showing the bookings, customers, revenue, and bookings-by-brand views your mockup already sketches out.

## Two apps, one shared source of truth

The updated prototype now shows this concretely: a Client App (what customers use to browse, book, and chat with the AI assistant) and an Admin App (what you and staff use to manage the business) as two distinct experiences, switchable from the top of the demo. The part that actually matters is not that they look different — it's that they both read from and write to the same underlying data. When you add a new product or service in the Admin App, it appears in the Client App immediately, because there is exactly one list of products and one list of services behind both interfaces, not two copies that can drift out of sync. In the prototype this shared data is simulated in the browser itself so you can see the link working live; in the real build, that same role is played by one database (the Supabase or Firebase option mentioned earlier) that both the Client App and the Admin App connect to over the internet, so a change one of your staff makes on a laptop shows up on a client's phone within seconds.

This is the right architecture to commit to from day one even if the Admin App's first version is very simple, because retrofitting "one shared source of truth" onto two apps that were each built with their own separate data is a much bigger job than building it that way from the start. The Admin App's Products and Services screens in the prototype are the actual shape this should take: a simple table of what exists, an "Add" button that opens a short form, and Edit and Delete on each row — this is deliberately unglamorous because data entry screens are a tool for you and your staff, not a showcase for clients, and the value is entirely in how fast and error-free entering a new product or adjusting a price is.

## Logins and roles, so reception isn't touching prices

The Admin App now opens behind a login, and each employee has their own account rather than everyone sharing one password. Access is no longer locked to three fixed roles — instead, the Owner shares access section by section: Overview, Bookings, Products, Services, Offers, Photos & Video, and Employees are each an individual checkbox on that person's account, so you can hand someone exactly the combination of sections their job actually needs, including combinations that don't fit a neat title (a senior stylist who should see Bookings and Photos & Video but not Products, say, or a part-time front-desk person who only needs Bookings). Three "Quick presets" — Owner/Admin (everything), Reception (Bookings only), and Staff/Provider (Bookings plus Photos & Video) — are offered as one-click starting points on the employee form because they cover the common cases, but they're a shortcut, not a constraint, and the checkboxes underneath can always be adjusted from there. On the login screen and in the Employees table, an account's access label still shows a familiar preset name when its checkboxes happen to match one exactly, and otherwise lists the sections it can reach (for example "Products, Services"), so it's always obvious at a glance what a given account can touch. This maps directly to how the front desk actually works day to day: reception needs to confirm and manage appointments constantly, but has no reason to be able to change what a Hydrafacial costs, and giving them that ability anyway is how prices quietly drift or get changed by accident. The bookings queue itself is real in the prototype too — when a client taps "Request via WhatsApp," it lands as an entry anyone with Bookings access can see and confirm, and the status updates (Requested → Confirmed → Completed) flow back to the client's own "My Bookings" screen, which is the same live-linked pattern used everywhere else in the app.

That caveat about this being only a demonstration no longer applies: **the staff login is now real.** It still looks the same — pick your name, type your PIN — but the PIN is checked by the database's own authentication service rather than compared against text in the file, so it cannot be read out of the page and a wrong PIN is refused by a server the browser does not control. Creating an employee in the Employees tab creates a genuine account behind the scenes, and PINs are now six digits rather than four, which matters for an account that can see every figure in the business.

Two things about it are worth knowing plainly. An owner still cannot reset a *different* employee's PIN — only that person can change their own, once logged in — because doing it for someone else needs a small piece of server-side code that has not been written yet. Doing it the easy way would mean putting the database's master key inside the app, where anyone could read it, and that is not a trade worth making. And "owner" is now its own checkbox, separate from having Employees access: someone can be allowed to add and edit staff without thereby being able to see every brand's takings. The app refuses to remove the last remaining owner, so there is no way to lock yourself out.

## Keeping each brand's staff out of the other two brands' data

Section access (Bookings, Products, Services, and so on) answers "which parts of the Admin App can this person open," but it doesn't answer "whose bookings, whose products, whose photos." A hair stylist who's been given Bookings access previously saw every booking across all three brands the moment they logged in — Dolce's clinic appointments included — which is fine for you as the owner moving between all three, but not appropriate for a Polished-only employee who has no reason to see a Core class roster or a Dolce client's injectables appointment. The employee form now has a second checkbox group underneath "Share these sections," labeled Department, with one box each for Dolce, Polished, and Core. Whichever brands are checked there become the only brands that account's Bookings, Products, Services, Offers, and Photos & Video ever show — the tables filter down automatically, the "Add" forms only offer that employee's own brand(s) in the brand dropdown, and the Services tab's Dolce/Polished/Core sub-tabs simply don't appear for a brand they don't have. A Dolce-only employee can no longer stumble into Polished's product list or a Core booking even by accident, because those records are never sent to their screen in the first place. Overview and Employees stay account-wide regardless of department, since those are about running the business as a whole rather than any one brand's day-to-day, and in practice the Owner is the account that typically holds both of those anyway.

In the demo data, Sara is scoped to Dolce only, Aya to Polished only, and a new demo account, Diyar, to Core only, so logging in as each one shows the restriction clearly side by side; the Owner account keeps all three departments checked, which is what "sees and manages everything" should mean for the person actually running all three brands. Department, like the section checkboxes above it, is a checkbox set rather than a fixed role, so nothing stops an employee who genuinely works across two brands — someone who does hair at Polished and also teaches a Core class, say — from having both boxes checked; it's additive, not exclusive. This is no longer only a matter of what the browser chooses to draw. The same department rule is now written into the database itself, so a Polished-only account asking the database directly for Dolce's bookings is refused by the database, not merely shown a filtered screen. That distinction is the whole point: a restricted employee cannot reach another brand's records through the browser's developer tools or any other route, because the records are never sent.

## Brand photos, and photos on products and services

Alongside all of that, the Owner's Overview tab now has a Brand Photos section — one upload slot each for Dolce, Polished, and Core — and the Product and Service edit forms each gained their own optional photo upload. Before this, every brand card, service row, and product tile fell back to a plain colour swatch, which is fine as a placeholder but was never meant to be the finished look. The fallback logic is layered sensibly: a service or product shows its own photo if one was uploaded, otherwise it borrows its brand's photo, and only falls back to a plain colour card if neither exists — so uploading just the three brand photos alone already makes the whole app look considerably more finished, and specific product or service photos can be filled in afterward as time allows rather than all at once.

Getting uploaded photos to actually *look* right took three rounds and turned up a real bug along the way. The brand cards set their colour using a CSS shorthand that silently cancelled the instruction to fill the card, so the moment a card had a photo it was shown at its original size, from the top-left corner, and tiled — which is why an uploaded picture looked broken rather than merely badly cropped. That is fixed.

With the photo filling the card properly, the remaining problem was *which part* of it each card shows. Cards are different shapes — a wide brand card, a square swatch in a list, a tall hero on the detail page — and all of them crop from the middle, so a head-and-shoulders portrait uploaded once would lose the head on the wide ones. The app now measures each photo to find its subject: flat areas like a wall or a backdrop score near zero, detailed areas score high, and skin tones count heavily, because in this business the subject of a photo is nearly always a person. Each card is then told where to crop from, so one upload lands correctly on every card shape with nothing thrown away. It also recognises a logo — a graphic sitting on a plain backdrop that runs to the edges — and fits it inside the card whole instead of cropping the wordmark in half, dropping the dark shading and the brand name that would otherwise sit on top of it.

That measurement is a good guess but still a guess, so every photo box in the Admin App also shows the picture inside the real card shapes and lets it be dragged into place with a mouse or a finger, with a button to go back to the automatic choice. The position is remembered against the picture itself rather than against the product, so a photo used in two places is framed once, and it travels to the other devices along with everything else.

## Editing the Upcoming Events & Offers strip

The promo chips on the welcome screen — "Glow Season," "Bridal Package," and so on — used to be fixed content baked into the app; now they're a proper Offers tab in the Admin App, following the exact same add/edit/delete pattern as Products and Services: a table of what's currently live, an "Add Offer" button that opens a short form (title, which brand it belongs to, a one-line description), and Edit and Delete on each row. Changes appear on the client's welcome screen immediately, the same live-linked behavior as everything else in the prototype. This is meant for the kind of thing that changes often — a seasonal discount, a workshop announcement, a holiday closure notice — without needing anyone to touch code, which matters because promotional content like this typically changes far more often than your core service list.

Products and Services now also carry their own optional discount percentage right on the price field, and this is deliberately separate from the manually-written Offers above — you don't create an Offer to announce that Balayage Color is 15% off; you set 15 in the Discount field on Balayage Color itself, and two things happen automatically: the price everywhere that service appears (the service list, its detail page, the booking screen, even the WhatsApp message text) shows the original price crossed out next to the discounted one with a small "-15%" badge, and it shows up on the welcome screen's Upcoming Events & Offers strip on its own, without being listed twice. The Offers tab shows these underneath the manual list, clearly separated and read-only, so it's obvious at a glance which promos are hand-written campaigns and which are just "this specific thing is currently discounted." Clearing the discount back to 0 removes it from the offers strip the same way deleting a manual offer would — nothing to remember to clean up separately.

## Managing the employee accounts themselves

Employees is its own tab, visible only to whoever has been given Employees access — hidden from everyone else the same way Products and Services already are. From there you can add a new employee (name, checkboxes for which sections they can reach, checkboxes for which brand or brands' data they can see, a PIN), edit an existing one — including changing their access if someone's job changes — and remove someone who's left. Resetting somebody else's PIN is the one thing this cannot do, for the reason given in the login section above; the person has to change their own once logged in. Two guardrails are built in on purpose: you can't delete the account you're currently logged in as, and the app won't let the last remaining account with Employees access be removed or have that box unchecked, so there's no way to accidentally lock everyone out of account management entirely. These are real accounts now rather than a demonstration, so adding someone here genuinely gives them a way in, and removing them genuinely takes it away.

## Daily accounting reports and exporting the data

A new Reports tab — visible only to whoever has Employees-level access, since it surfaces revenue — gives you a daily accounting view and a full export of everything. The daily report picks a date (today by default), lists every booking on it with its price (respecting any discount, the same price the client actually saw), and totals it up by brand and grand total, with cancelled bookings excluded from the totals but still listed so nothing silently disappears from the record. If you're logged in as a department-scoped employee rather than the Owner, the report — like every other section — only shows their brand's bookings.

Underneath that, "Export All Data" pulls together every booking, product, service, offer and (for accounts with Employees access) employee record this login can see, in one file — the same use-you'd-hand-to-an-accountant idea, just everything rather than one day. Both the daily report and the full export can be saved as an Excel file or a PDF. The Excel file is actually a CSV under the hood, which Excel, Numbers and Google Sheets all open natively — a real .xlsx file needs a code library to generate, and pulling one in would either require the app to reach the internet (defeating the point of the offline Android test build) or roughly double the size of a single HTML file for a feature most people will only use occasionally. The PDF works the same practical way: "Export PDF" opens a clean, print-only version of the report and hands it to your browser's own print dialog, where choosing "Save as PDF" produces a real PDF — no library, works completely offline, and is exactly how most business software generates PDFs under the hood anyway. If a future version wants a native .xlsx or a more polished branded PDF, that's straightforward to add once there's a real backend generating these server-side, which is also the point at which the underlying numbers stop being demo data and start being real.

## Showing prices in USD or Iraqi Dinar

Every price in the app — services, products, purchases, register totals, all of it — is still entered and stored in USD everywhere; that part never changes, and it's what keeps the accounting features above unambiguous. What's new is a display toggle in the top navigation bar, right next to the language switch, that lets anyone viewing the app — client or admin — see those same USD prices converted to Iraqi Dinar instead, formatted the way IQD actually reads ("174,000 IQD" rather than a decimal). It's genuinely just a different way of looking at the same numbers: switch it back to USD and every figure returns to exactly what it was, because nothing was ever converted and re-stored — only displayed differently.

The exchange rate itself lives on the Overview tab as an editable field ("Exchange rate — IQD per 1 USD"), because the USD/IQD rate is a market rate that moves, not a fixed peg — you update it yourself whenever it's worth updating, and the app has no live feed checking it for you. One deliberate limitation, flagged directly in the register-close form itself: the "Counted cash" and "Counted card" fields when closing the register always take a USD number, even while the display toggle is set to IQD — I kept that boundary firm on purpose, since letting the same input field sometimes mean USD and sometimes mean IQD depending on a toggle is exactly the kind of ambiguity that causes real bookkeeping mistakes. In practice that means: if you're counting actual Dinar notes from the till, do the division by the day's rate yourself before typing the USD-equivalent total in — a small inconvenience worth it for the two currencies never getting silently confused inside the stored numbers. The CSV and PDF exports (daily report and full export) also always stay in USD regardless of the toggle, for the same reason — an accountant working from an export wants one consistent currency throughout, not whatever a viewer happened to have selected on screen at the time.

## Inventory, restocking, and closing the register

You asked at one point about turning this into a full accounting system — purchases, warehouses, showrooms, the works. I want to be honest about where I drew the line and why, since it shapes everything below: real accounting software (QuickBooks, Xero, Zoho Books, or a local Iraqi/KRG package) already solves double-entry bookkeeping and tax compliance properly, with years of correctness behind it that a from-scratch rebuild inside a single HTML file, with no real backend, would not match. What I built instead is the operational layer that *feeds* an accountant, not a replacement for one — the same instinct as the exports above, just further upstream.

Products now track **stock on hand**, editable directly on the product form or moved automatically by the two flows around it: **Restocking** (called Purchases until it was renamed, because "Purchases" reads naturally as *what customers bought*, which is the opposite of what the tab is for), a tab where you log buying stock in from a supplier (product, quantity, unit cost, supplier name, date) which adds straight to that product's stock, and **Sell**, a button on each product row in the Products tab that records a retail sale (quantity, optional client name) and removes it from stock — both leave a paper trail rather than just quietly changing a number. The Products table itself flags anything at 5 or fewer as "low," and anything at zero as "Out of stock" with the Sell button disabled, so a glance at the tab tells you what needs reordering.

The Reports tab's daily total now includes product sales alongside bookings, and underneath it there's an actual **end-of-day register close** — the thing "daily report" was missing before. Pick a date, and if it hasn't been closed yet, you enter what was actually counted (cash and card separately) against the expected total, add a note if there's a discrepancy, and close it. Once closed, that day's bookings can no longer be advanced or cancelled from the Bookings tab — they show "🔒 Day closed" instead — so the books for a closed day don't quietly keep changing after you've gone home. Reopening a closed day is possible, but only for accounts with Employees access, and it's a deliberate action (a visible "Reopen this day" button), not something that happens by accident — this mirrors how real accounting software treats a closed period. Closing respects department the same way everything else does: a department-scoped employee closes only their own brand's register, while the Owner can close all three brands in one action; the Bookings and Restocking tabs, and the full data export, all picked up equivalent new sections so nothing about this is hidden from the exports you'd hand to an accountant.

What this deliberately does *not* do: no warehouses or multiple stock locations (one stock number per product, since three brands sharing space doesn't need separate site tracking yet), no chart of accounts or general ledger, no automatic tax calculation, and no purchase orders or supplier invoicing beyond the simple restock log. If the business grows into needing those, that's the point at which a real accounting package should sit alongside this app (fed by the same exports), rather than this app trying to become one.

## Photos and video: before/after and progress

The prototype now includes this too, and it's built on the same pattern as products and services: staff upload a photo or short video from the Admin App's Photos & Video tab, tag it to a brand and (optionally) a specific service, and it appears instantly on the client side. The one addition that matters here is a visibility choice on every upload — public, meaning it shows on that service's page for any visitor to see (a before/after pair, a process shot), or private, meaning it only shows in that one client's own "My Progress" view. Public uploads require a consent checkbox before they can be saved, which is a deliberate friction point: it forces the person entering data to actively confirm the client agreed to public use rather than defaulting to "public" and hoping someone remembers to ask. In the real build this consent flag should be paired with a client's actual name or booking record rather than typed free text, and you'll want a simple way for a client to later ask that a specific photo be taken down — worth a short conversation with a lawyer about what the Kurdistan Region and Iraq require here, since this is health-adjacent data once Dolce is involved.

## Aftercare: a note to the client when the treatment ends

A client who has just had a Hydrafacial is the one person most likely to read, and act on, a short note about wearing sunblock — and far more likely on the day than three days later. So each treatment now carries its own **aftercare note**, written on the service itself in the Admin App, and the moment reception marks that booking Completed — which they do anyway as the client leaves — the note appears on screen addressed to that client, with a button that opens WhatsApp with the message already written.

Three things must be true or nothing is offered: the treatment has a note written for it, the client agreed to be contacted, and there is a number to send to. Consent is asked for at the point of booking, either by the client themselves in the app or by reception at the desk, and recorded with the booking. A treatment with no note written simply never sends anything, so the notes can be added one at a time as your doctors write them rather than all at once.

Two details are deliberate. The message opens with "a note from Dolce about looking after your results" rather than naming the treatment, because a WhatsApp notification shows on a lock screen and not everything a clinic does is something a client wants read over someone's shoulder; the detail sits inside the message. And the prompt names which brand's WhatsApp account the message should go out from, with the number, because the app can choose who the message goes *to* but not who it comes *from* — that depends on which account is signed in on the device in reception's hand, and a client replying "my skin is red" needs it to reach the clinic rather than someone's personal phone.

The wording of the notes themselves is written by the clinic's own doctors, not generated by the app, and the field ships empty for exactly that reason. It is general aftercare that goes to every client having that treatment, so it cannot carry advice meant for one person, and anyone replying with a problem has to reach a human quickly.

This is one tap short of automatic. Making it send with nobody touching anything needs the WhatsApp Business API — the same approval process described under client accounts — but the trigger, the rules and the messages are all built, so when that clears it becomes a small change rather than a new feature.

## The app on a phone: Dolce+ on iPhone and Android

The same single file that runs the website is now also wrapped as a real installable app, named **Dolce+**, on both iPhone and Android. The native build deliberately shows only the client app — no marketing page, no phone-shaped mockup, no admin toggle — so what someone sees after installing is the app itself, opening on a short animation of the Dolce+ logo, with the Core and Polished logos appearing when those brands are opened. Android's back button behaves the way Android users expect: it closes an open photo or panel first, and steps back through the screens rather than dumping the person out of the app.

An Android installer file can be built on demand and sent to a phone directly, which is how it has been tested on a Galaxy Fold. That file is fine for you and your staff; putting either app in the App Store or Play Store needs a signed release build and a developer account in your name, which is yours to create when you decide to publish.

## One shared database, so every device sees the same thing

Until now each device kept its own private copy of everything. If the front desk added a product on the iPad, it existed only on that iPad; the owner's phone knew nothing about it. That is now fixed. All three brands share one cloud database, and every device reads from and writes to it, so a price changed in the morning is the price the customer sees on their phone in the afternoon, wherever it was changed from.

The important design decision is that the app never waits for the internet. Every change is saved to the device it was made on the instant it is made, exactly as before, and only then sent to the shared database. If the connection is slow, patchy or gone entirely, nothing stalls and nothing is lost: the change goes into a queue, a small marker appears in the corner saying how many changes are still waiting, and they are sent automatically the moment the connection returns — including after the app has been closed and reopened. What the queue remembers is which record changed rather than a copy of it, so if someone edits a price three times while offline, one update is sent at the end carrying the final price, not three stale ones arguing with each other.

Security is enforced by the database itself rather than only by what the app chooses to show. Every rule about who may see or change what is written into the database, so a receptionist scoped to Dolce cannot read Polished's takings even in principle, a member of staff without the Products section cannot change a price, and a member of the public with no login can see the service list and request a booking but cannot read the diary or pull out the customer list. Those rules were tested by simulating each type of account and confirming that thirty-nine separate things that should be impossible actually are. The database also refuses records that make no sense regardless of which app sent them — a gallery photo marked public without consent ticked, a negative stock count, a discount over one hundred percent.

There is one moment worth understanding, which is the very first time the shared database is switched on. It starts completely empty, and normally the shared database is treated as the single source of truth. Left alone that would wipe every booking and record already sitting on the device. So the app checks: if the shared database has nothing in it at all, it leaves the device alone, and if the person logging in is the owner, it uploads that device's existing bookings, purchases, sales, register closes, customers and photos to fill it — once, and only once. After that the shared database is genuinely the master copy and normal syncing takes over.

One thing is deliberately still to come: photographs are stored inside the database itself, which works but makes the catalog heavier to download as the gallery grows, and their proper home is dedicated file storage with only a link kept in the database. Customer accounts are a separate question, settled for now in favour of guest booking and described further up.

Two practical matters that belong here. The database provider keeps **no backups at all** on the free plan, so if records are deleted by mistake there is nothing to recover from. An owner-only **Save a backup file** button in the Overview tab writes everything — bookings, customers, sales, register history, photos — to a single file that can be restored later; taking one regularly, onto a drive that is not the same laptop, is the whole safety net until the paid plan is worth its cost. Those files contain real customer names and phone numbers, so they want an encrypted drive rather than a shared USB stick or an email attachment.

The second is that when a change cannot be sent to the shared database, the app no longer fails quietly. A red marker appears with the count, and it can be opened to see exactly which records are stuck and why, with buttons to try again or to clear them. A sync failure you cannot see is worse than one you can.

## A phased build, so you are never blocked waiting for "the whole thing"

The first phase is the smallest version that is still genuinely useful: the destination picker, service browsing and pricing for all three brands, service detail screens, and booking with a real date/time picker (in-app and via WhatsApp, as the prototype now shows), plus English and Arabic (adding Kurdish shortly after). This alone replaces having three separate Instagram-DM-based booking processes with one coherent, professional experience, and it can realistically be live within a few weeks once content (service lists, prices, photos) is ready. The second phase adds the AI assistant with real recommendations, the loyalty program, and the retail shop — this is where the product starts to feel genuinely different from a normal clinic or salon website, because it actively works to cross-sell between your three brands. The third phase adds the admin dashboard with full analytics, Kurdish language support if not already included, a proper native app wrapper for the App Store and Play Store, and true staff-calendar-aware scheduling — availability that accounts for which specific provider is free, not just whether the time slot is taken by anyone.

Throughout, the highest-leverage early investment is not code — it is getting a clean, structured list of every service, price, duration, and description across all three brands into one place (a spreadsheet is fine to start), because that same data feeds the app's service pages, the AI assistant's knowledge base, and the admin dashboard. Good source data makes every later phase faster and makes the AI assistant meaningfully more accurate from day one.

## What I'd suggest as next steps

The app is past the prototype stage. It is published, it runs on a phone as an installed app, it takes real bookings from real customers, staff log in against real accounts, and the database enforces who may see what rather than trusting the browser. What remains divides into things only you can do and decisions still open.

The things only you can do, in the order they matter: **upload real treatment photos**, which is now the single biggest gap between how the app works and how it looks, and the one item nobody else can supply; **have your doctors write the aftercare notes** for the treatments that need them; **have a native speaker read the Kurdish and Arabic** on the new booking form, since that wording has not been checked by anyone who speaks them; and **take a backup**, then keep taking them.

The decisions still open are the two described earlier — whether customer accounts are worth building at all, and whether to begin the WhatsApp Business API approvals, which would make the aftercare messages fully automatic and correctly branded as well as making WhatsApp login possible. Both are reasonable to defer; neither should be forgotten.

Beyond that, the accounting work sketched in this document is scoped but unbuilt: recording expenses, making the dashboard show real figures rather than placeholders, and locking a service's price to what it was when the booking was made rather than what it is today. That is the natural next block of building once the items above are settled.
